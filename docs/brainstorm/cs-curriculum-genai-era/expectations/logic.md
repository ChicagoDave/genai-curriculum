# Expectation: Logic

**Status**: IN (foundation: the faculty every other expectation runs on; taught in year one through play)
**Time box**: foundation, year one, as the math requirement's CS-facing form (Q-4, decided 2026-09-25)
**Named discipline**: — (logic itself; taught through games, puzzles, proofs, and innovation exercises, decision college-0012)
**Dived**: 2026-09-27

Roster candidate 17, the last dived and the odd one. No corporate acceptor asks for logic by name. It is held to through every other expectation: a behavior statement is a precondition and a postcondition, an invariant is a proposition that must hold, a specification is a predicate, a test is a claim with a truth value, a contract is an agreement on terms, and reading structure against intent is checking a design for consistency. The overview calls it the premium faculty, the one that judges whether the agent's output is right. Settled 2026-09-27: **it is taught through play**, and it is potentially the most fun part of the degree. Board, text, and video games; logic puzzles; geometry proofs; innovation exercises. Its membership verdict is a fourth category the template did not need until now: **foundation** (decision college-0012).

## What the corporate acceptor holds the graduate to

**Nothing by name, and everything by consequence.** No acceptor states "the graduate must reason correctly." Every acceptor holds the graduate to artifacts that are wrong if the reasoning is: a REJECTS WHEN that misses a case, an invariant that does not actually constrain, a test that passes for the wrong reason, a matrix whose weights contradict its scenario, a reading that accepts a structure that could not satisfy its own design. Logic is the difference between a graduate who can author those artifacts and one who can only fill in the form.

**Why year one and why play.** The student has no system yet and writes no code ever. What they need before the first slice is the faculty, not the vocabulary, and the faculty is built by exercising it on things that push back: a game whose rules can be broken, a puzzle with one answer, a proof that either holds or does not, an exercise with no right answer that still has better and worse ones. Each activity builds a part of what year two will need:

| Activity | What it exercises | What it prepares for |
|---|---|---|
| **Board games** | Rules as constraints that hold on every turn; state; legal and illegal moves; strategy under rules | Invariants (modularization), REJECTS WHEN (testing, validation), turns and hand-offs (workflows) |
| **Text games** | A world with state; stating intent and comparing it to what happened; the parser's literal reading of an instruction | Scenarios in the people's language, design against intent (reading code), the agent's literal reading of a statement |
| **Video games** | Systems with feedback loops; resource constraints; failure and recovery; emergent behavior from simple rules | Operability and resilience, performance under load, collisions between expectations |
| **Logic puzzles** | Deduction under constraint; quantifiers; contradiction; the case nobody stated | Necessary and sufficient conditions, the gap the professor sets and waits on (decision college-0008), specification as a predicate |
| **Geometry proofs** | The shape of a proof: givens, steps, a conclusion that follows; a claim that holds for every case, not most | Tests as verdicts, an invariant that actually constrains, induction as the shape of "for every booking" |
| **Innovation exercises** | Generating alternatives; lateral moves; constraints as prompts; better and worse without right and wrong | Option evaluation (candidate 19), design judgment, the Alternatives matrix (decision college-0006) |

Two halves in one year: the **rigorous** half (puzzles, proofs, the rules of games) builds the faculty that judges, and the **generative** half (innovation exercises, designing games, playing to win) builds the faculty that proposes. Design judgment needs both; the degree has tended to teach only the first, and only as symbol manipulation.

## What the graduate must be able to state

No slice exists in year one, so the artifacts are the play's own:

- **A game's rules as constraints**, written so that a move is unambiguously legal or not, and the state a move changes. The student's first system, with no software: the rules are invariants, the state is the model, the turns are the workflow.
- **A puzzle's solution with its reasoning**, step by step, including the case that had to be ruled out. Not the answer; the argument.
- **A proof**, in geometry or another domain that pushes back, from givens to conclusion with every step justified, and a stated understanding of why one counter-case breaks it.
- **What went wrong in a text game**, in two sentences: what was intended, what the world did, and where the instruction's literal reading diverged from the intent. This is the sentence the year-two student will write about the agent's output.
- **Several alternatives to a stated problem**, with a reason each is better or worse against a constraint, from an innovation exercise. The matrix without the form.
- **A contradiction found**, in a game's rules, a puzzle's premises, or a proof's steps, stated as which two things cannot both hold. Skepticism as a habit before there is anything to be skeptical of (decision college-0008).

## What proves it in a slice

There is no slice in year one. The proofs are the play's:

- **A game designed and played.** The student, or a small group, writes a game's rules as constraints and the cohort plays it. It passes when no legal-or-not dispute arises that the rules do not settle, or when one does and the student amends the rule and names what it failed to constrain. The proposed year-one capstone, and the first slice without software (flagged below).
- **A puzzle set, reasoning graded.** Right answers with wrong reasoning fail. A wrong answer with a sound argument and one misread premise passes better.
- **A proof defended.** A written proof, then a counter-case offered by the professor or a peer, and the student says whether it breaks the proof and why.
- **A text game played and narrated.** Intent, result, and the divergence, written after each session. The habit of comparing what was meant to what was built, learned where the stakes are a locked door.
- **An innovation exercise with alternatives ranked.** Several candidates against a constraint, with reasons. Graded on the spread of the alternatives and the honesty of the reasons, not on the pick.

## Collisions

Logic collides with everything by design; these are the specific ones:

- **Workflows / requirements / scenarios**: text games are scenarios with a world model and a literal-minded executor, which is exactly what the agent will be in year two.
- **Modularization**: a game's rules are invariants; a game with one rule is decision college-0003's one thing with one invariant, met a year early on a board.
- **Testing**: a proof is a claim that holds for every case; a test is a claim that holds for one and is trusted for the rest only because the reasoning says so.
- **Data validation**: a specification is a predicate, and the puzzle habit of stating what must be true for a case to qualify is the same habit.
- **Reading code (candidate 18)**: design against intent is the text-game narration applied to the agent's structure.
- **Option evaluation (candidate 19)**: innovation exercises are the generative half of the matrix; the year-one student learns to produce alternatives before learning to weigh them.
- **Decision college-0008**: puzzles are where challenging a premise is first rewarded, and a professor who sets a gap and waits is playing the same game.
- **Q-4**: stands. Foundation, year one, as the math requirement's CS-facing form. This dive supplies the how.
- **Decision college-0001's template**: the membership verdict gains a fourth category, foundation, for this candidate alone (decision college-0012).

## Membership verdict

**Foundation.** Not an expectation, because no acceptor states it; not a discipline, because it is not a named way of meeting one; not a skill exercised across expectations, because it is what every skill runs on. The fourth category exists for this row. The pedagogy is play: games, puzzles, proofs, and innovation exercises, chosen because they push back, and because the faculty is built by exercising it on things that do.

## Expansion budget

**Year one, in full, then never as a unit again.** After year one, logic is not expanded; it is exercised, in every behavior statement, invariant, test, matrix, and reading. The rigorous half and the generative half are both present in year one; neither is deferred.

## Open in this dive

- **The acceptor for the foundation year.** Whether the math department accepts it as satisfying the math requirement, or this curriculum's own faculty owns it and the requirement is met another way. Q-4 says it is the math requirement's CS-facing form; who signs off is not decided.
- **The game-design capstone.** A game written as rules and played by the cohort as the year's proof, and the first slice without software. Proposed; not decided.
- **Which games.** Board, text, and video games are the categories; titles and the balance among them are the professor's, per cohort.
